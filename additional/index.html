About Me
Professional Bio

I'm Denys Shlyakhtovskyy, a final-year Electrical Engineering student at the University of British Columbia Okanagan, with a focus on RF systems, wireless communications, and embedded hardware. Over the course of my degree I've been drawn to problems that sit at the intersection of analog and digital — where signals leave the board and have to survive the real world.

My academic background spans electromagnetics, signal processing, power electronics, and digital systems design. Outside of coursework I've pursued hands-on experience wherever I could find it — from servicing mission-critical RF repeater infrastructure for Alberta Wildfire Communications to diagnosing field faults on remote communications sites accessible only by helicopter. That work reinforced something lectures can't fully teach: systems fail in unexpected ways, and good engineering is as much about discipline and documentation as it is about design.

Professionally, I'm aiming toward a career in RF/wireless systems engineering — whether that's in communications infrastructure, defence electronics, or emerging wireless standards. I'm drawn to roles where the hardware and the physics still matter.

Personal Interests

Outside of engineering I spend a lot of time in the mountains — hiking, skiing, and the occasional backcountry trip. Spending time in remote terrain gives me a genuine appreciation for reliable communications systems; a broken link in the backcountry isn't an inconvenience, it's a safety issue. That perspective bleeds directly into how I think about system reliability and redundancy at work.

Reflection
Personal Reflection

Going into this course, I thought I had a decent understanding of what engineering design looked like. Coming out of it, I realize I had a fairly idealized picture. The design process, at least as I experienced it this term, is iterative in a way that's uncomfortable if you're expecting a straight line from requirements to solution. You make a decision, test it, find out it doesn't behave the way you expected, and then you adapt. That cycle repeated more times than I anticipated, and I think that's the most honest thing I can say I learned: iteration isn't a sign that something went wrong, it's just how engineering actually works.

As for what makes a good engineer — I used to put technical knowledge at the top of that list. I still think it matters, but after this project I'd put communication and intellectual honesty ahead of it. You have to be able to explain what you're doing and why, to your teammates, to your client, and to yourself. And you have to be willing to say "this isn't working" early enough to do something about it, rather than defending a design decision past the point where it's clearly not serving the goal. Those turned out to be harder skills than any circuit I had to design.

This project also shifted how I think about the professional engineer's role in society. Engineers make decisions with real consequences — for safety, for the environment, for the communities that use what we build. That sounds abstract until you're sitting across from an industry client explaining why your prototype missed a spec. At that point it stops being theoretical. The PE stamp exists for a reason, and I have more respect for what it represents now than I did at the start of the program.

Looking at my own development, the strengths I'm most confident in are systematic troubleshooting, attention to detail at the component and circuit level, and an ability to work through ambiguity without needing everything mapped out in advance. Where I need to grow is in project management — specifically, breaking large deliverables into smaller milestones early enough that slippage in one area doesn't cascade. I also want to get better at presenting technical work to non-technical audiences; that's a skill I've started developing but haven't yet had enough practice with. Going forward, I plan to actively seek roles and projects that put me in front of clients and mixed audiences so I can build that muscle deliberately, rather than hoping it develops on its own.
